The central mistake in mystery writing is starting from the beginning.

Most writers open with a crime and then improvise forward, discovering who did it, how, and why as they write. This produces mysteries with endings that feel unearned, clues that contradict each other, and solutions that require the detective to know things they could not possibly have observed.

The writers who finish mysteries and end them well work backward. They begin with the solution and construct everything else as the machinery that delivers the reader to that conclusion while making them feel they could have solved it themselves.

## Know Whodunit Before You Write Chapter One

This is not optional. You cannot plant fair clues to a solution you have not decided on. You cannot foreshadow an explanation you are still inventing. You cannot control what your detective notices (and what they miss) unless you already know the shape of the truth they are moving toward.

Write the solution first. Write it as if explaining it to someone: who committed the crime, how they did it, why they did it, and how they concealed it. Make it specific. The vaguer the solution is in your own mind, the worse the mystery will be.

Once you have the solution, work backward through the events leading to the crime. What did the perpetrator do to prepare? What evidence did they leave without knowing it? What evidence did they think they destroyed but didn't? Who else was present, and what do they know?

This is your mystery's actual structure. The detective's investigation is the story you build on top of it.

## The Fair Play Contract

Mystery readers are playing a game with the author. The rules are simple: every clue necessary to solve the mystery must be available to the reader before the solution is revealed. Nothing in the explanation can depend on information the reader never had access to.

This is the fair play contract, and it is not negotiable. Mystery readers who feel cheated do not give second chances. A solution that depends on information the author withheld (forensic details the detective kept to themselves, a witness who was never introduced, a motive that appeared in the final chapter) is a solution that breaks the contract.

The practical implication: every element of your solution needs to appear in the story before the denouement, in plain sight. The reader should be able to go back and find every piece of the answer in the pages before it was revealed.

This is harder than it sounds, because it requires you to hide the significance of clues while displaying them clearly. The reader must see the detail without knowing it matters. This is the central craft challenge of mystery writing.

## Burying Clues in Plain Sight

A clue is invisible when the reader has no reason to attach significance to it. There are three techniques for achieving this:

**Embed clues in character.** The most effective hiding places are human behaviours. A character who arrives ten minutes late to every scene. A character who never makes eye contact. A character who asks one question too many about the victim's schedule. These details read as characterisation. They are also evidence.

**Surround the clue with noise.** A significant detail is less visible when surrounded by insignificant ones. If you describe a room in which one object is the key clue, describe fifteen objects with equal attention. The reader's eye sweeps across the list. The important thing goes in the middle.

**Give the clue a misleading explanation.** The reader encounters a detail and immediately reaches for an explanation. If you provide one (a plausible one that is wrong), they will accept it and move on. The real significance of the detail stays hidden because the reader has already explained it away.

## Red Herrings and Why Most Writers Use Them Wrong

A red herring is a false lead. It is not a random piece of misdirection. It is a deliberate construction that gives the reader a reason to suspect someone or something that turns out to be innocent or irrelevant.

The mistake most writers make with red herrings is inventing them to confuse the reader rather than to serve the story. A red herring that exists only to mislead is a cheat. When it is exposed as false, the reader feels manipulated, not engaged.

A good red herring does two things simultaneously: it misdirects the investigation, and it reveals character or advances another plot element. A suspect who appears guilty because they are hiding something (not the crime, but their own secret) gives the detective and the reader the wrong answer while delivering true information about the world of the story.

The red herring must be emotionally satisfying to pursue even after it is disproven.

## The Detective's Knowledge Problem

Your detective knows only what they have observed, been told, or correctly deduced. Nothing else.

This sounds obvious. It is surprisingly easy to violate. When you know the solution, you know things the detective does not yet know. That knowledge bleeds into how you write their observations. They notice things they have no reason to notice. They draw conclusions that require evidence they do not have. The internal logic breaks.

Read your detective's scenes with this question: what, exactly, do they know at this point, and how do they know it? If they know something they should not know yet, the scene needs to be revised.

The detective's journey should be a genuine process of reasoning from incomplete information toward the truth. The reader should be able to follow that reasoning, and should in theory be able to conduct the same investigation and arrive at the same conclusion, even if they do not.

## The Timeline Is Everything

Mystery plots run on two timelines simultaneously: the timeline of the crime (what actually happened, in order) and the timeline of the investigation (what the detective discovers, and when).

Both timelines need to be internally consistent. The crime timeline especially must be scrutinised for plausibility. Can the perpetrator have been in two places at the time they claimed? Does the forensic evidence match the timeline as you have constructed it? Do witness accounts of timing align with the established facts?

Write both timelines out explicitly before you begin drafting. A table works well: on one side, the actual sequence of events in the crime. On the other, the sequence of the investigation. They are the same events seen from two different directions.

## Keeping Track of What Each Character Knows

In a mystery, different characters know different subsets of the truth. The perpetrator knows everything. The victim's family knows only what they witnessed. The detective accumulates knowledge across the investigation. Secondary characters have partial information.

This creates a complex information ecology that you need to track carefully. A character who reveals something they could not know has blown the solution. A character who hides something they have no reason to hide signals the reader that they are significant.

Maintain a record for each character of consequence: what they know, when they learned it, and what they are concealing. This is not optional for a plot-logic-dependent genre. When you have fifty pages of manuscript and ten characters, you can hold this in your head. When you have two hundred pages and twenty characters, you cannot.

## Writing the Reveal

The reveal is the most technically difficult scene in any mystery. It must:

- Explain the solution in a way the reader can follow
- Demonstrate that every clue was present and findable
- Create emotional satisfaction, not just intellectual resolution
- Not feel like a lecture

The most common failure is the information dump, where the detective stands in a room and explains everything for five pages. The explanation is correct, logically complete, and utterly lifeless.

The reveal works best when it is dramatised rather than explained. The detective does not describe what happened. They reconstruct it in a space where the physical and emotional stakes are still active. Someone in the room is the perpetrator, and they know it. That pressure transforms exposition into drama.

Write the reveal scene last. Once you know the full shape of the mystery, the reveal is the scene you have been building toward. It should read as inevitable: every clue, every misdirection, every red herring pointing in one direction once you know where to look.

## The Thing Underneath the Crime

The best mysteries are not about the crime. They are about something else: a family secret, a community's hypocrisy, a character's failure to protect someone they should have protected. The crime is the vehicle for that revelation.

The investigation is the process of uncovering a deeper truth. The solution is the answer to two questions simultaneously: who committed the crime, and what does that tell us about the world these people live in?

Without that second question, a mystery is a puzzle. With it, it is a novel.
