## Post 07 - Why Writers Quit at Chapter 4

**Topic:** Writing psychology
**Best day to post:** Monday

### LinkedIn

Most writers who start novels quit around chapter 4.

Not because the story is bad. Because the initial excitement has worn off and the difficulty has not shown up yet in the outline.

Chapter 4 is where the gap between what you imagined and what you are writing becomes impossible to ignore. The voice is not quite right. The pacing feels off. The character seems thinner on the page than they did in your head.

This is not a sign that the novel is broken. It is a sign that you are past the easy part.

The writers who finish do not have better ideas. They have a different relationship with difficulty. They have learned that resistance in the draft is normal and that pushing through it is the job.

Lower your standards for the first draft. Just get it down. You can fix it later. You cannot fix a blank page.

## Post 08 - Writing Through a Stuck Scene

**Topic:** Practical craft
**Best day to post:** Wednesday

### LinkedIn

When a scene is not working, most writers assume they need to write better.

Usually they need to write differently.

A few things worth trying when you are stuck:

1. Write the scene from a different character's point of view. Even if you do not use it. The shift reveals what the scene is actually about.

2. Ask what your character wants in this specific scene and what is stopping them. If nothing is stopping them, the scene does not have conflict. That is why it feels flat.

3. Skip the scene entirely and write the next one. Sometimes a scene makes sense only after you know where you are going.

4. Write the scene badly on purpose. Give yourself permission to put down something rough. The good version often hides just behind it.

Being stuck is usually a sign that the scene is trying to tell you something about what it needs to be.

## Post 09 - Managing Subplots

**Topic:** Plot structure
**Best day to post:** Friday

### LinkedIn

Subplots are one of the most powerful tools in long-form fiction.

They are also one of the easiest to mishandle.

A subplot earns its place when it reflects, complicates, or contrasts with the main story's themes. The secondary romance that mirrors what the protagonist cannot admit they want. The side character whose parallel journey illuminates what the hero is refusing to see.

A subplot wastes space when it just exists. Events happen. Characters have conversations. Nothing connects back.

Before you write a subplot, ask: what would be missing from the story if this was not here? If the answer is "some scenes," it is not a subplot. If the answer is "something essential about the theme," you have found your reason to write it.

## Post 10 - The Character Rename Problem

**Topic:** Revision + platform insight
**Best day to post:** Tuesday

### LinkedIn

We fixed a specific bug this week that taught me something about novel writing.

A user renamed a main character across their entire manuscript. Common task. Totally normal part of the revision process.

But here is what a simple find-and-replace does not catch:

- The character's name used as a possessive ("Marcus's coat")
- First-person references when the character is the narrator
- Dialogue attribution ("said Marcus" vs "Marcus said")
- Pronoun chains where the name is not mentioned but the character is the antecedent

Four instances slipped through in this user's manuscript. Their editor caught two. A reader caught one. One lived in the published version.

When we built the character rename feature, we had to teach it grammar, not just string matching. It is one of the more interesting engineering problems in this space.

## Post 11 - Beta Readers vs Editors

**Topic:** Publishing process
**Best day to post:** Thursday

### LinkedIn

Beta readers and editors are not the same thing. Mixing them up wastes both.

Beta readers are readers first. They tell you what lands, what confuses them, where they got bored, what they did not believe. They respond to the story as an experience. They are valuable before you revise.

Editors (line editors specifically) look at the prose. Sentence structure, word choice, rhythm, redundancy. They work at the craft level, not the story level. They are valuable after the story is already working.

Sending a beta reader your manuscript to get line notes is asking them to do a job they did not sign up for.

Sending a manuscript to a line editor before the structure is solid is burning money fixing sentences that might get cut anyway.

Use them in order. Story first. Then prose.

## Post 12 - The Revision Trap

**Topic:** Writing habits
**Best day to post:** Saturday

### LinkedIn

Some writers never finish their novel because they never stop revising the first act.

They write chapters 1 through 3. Revise them. Write chapter 4. Go back and revise 1 through 4. Write chapter 5. Revise again.

By the time they reach chapter 10, they have a nearly perfect first act and no novel.

The issue is that early revision gives you the feeling of progress without requiring you to face the harder parts of the draft.

Write the whole thing first. It does not have to be good. You need to know the ending before you can revise the beginning, because what you are setting up in chapter 1 depends entirely on what you are paying off in chapter 40.

First drafts are for discovering the story. Revision is for telling it.
