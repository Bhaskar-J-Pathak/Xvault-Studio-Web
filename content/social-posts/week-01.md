## Post 01 - Why Most Novel Outlines Fail

**Topic:** Outlining
**Best day to post:** Monday

### LinkedIn

Most novel outlines fail before the story does.

Not because writers don't plan enough. Because they plan the wrong things.

A typical outline answers: what happens in each chapter.

But that doesn't help you when you're stuck in chapter 8 wondering why your protagonist would ever do this.

The outline questions that actually matter:

- What does your protagonist want, and what do they fear losing even more?
- What is the worst possible moment you can put them in?
- What has to change in them by the end?

If you can answer those three, chapter 8 unlocks itself.

The plot is not the story. The character is the story. Outline the character first.

## Post 03 - The Wound

**Topic:** Character craft
**Best day to post:** Friday

### LinkedIn

Every character needs a wound.

Not a dramatic backstory. Not a trauma checklist. A wound: the unresolved thing that shapes every decision they make without them knowing it.

A character who was abandoned will push people away before they can leave. A character who was humiliated will overcompensate with pride. A character who was powerless will seek control, sometimes in ways that hurt the people they love.

The wound is not backstory. It lives in the present tense of your story.

If your character feels flat, you probably know what happened to them. You just have not decided how it broke them and how it drives them now.

That is the missing piece.

## Post 04 - Plot Threads and How They Disappear

**Topic:** Plot management
**Best day to post:** Tuesday

### LinkedIn

You planted a mysterious object in chapter 3.

You had a plan for it. It felt important. A careful reader would catch it.

By chapter 22, you forgot about it completely.

This happens to almost every novelist writing a first draft. Not because they are careless. Because the brain is managing so many things at once: character voice, scene momentum, pacing, dialogue, continuity, plot logic.

A thread you planted 60,000 words ago is genuinely hard to hold onto.

The fix is not to write slower. It is to track.

Keep a running list of every open question your manuscript raises. Things introduced that have not resolved yet. Check it every few chapters. You will catch the forgotten objects before your editor does.

## Post 05 - Voice Matters More Than Vocabulary

**Topic:** Writing voice
**Best day to post:** Thursday

### LinkedIn

Writers often think improving their prose means improving their vocabulary.

It doesn't.

Readers don't notice words. They notice rhythm. The length of your sentences. The details you choose to include and the ones you leave out. Whether your narrator sounds like someone they would trust.

Voice is not what you say. It is how you would say it if you were the only person in the room.

The writers with the most distinctive voices are often not using unusual words. Cormac McCarthy. Raymond Carver. They are making consistent choices about what goes in and what stays out.

The best thing you can do for your voice is write a lot without editing. Then read it aloud. Your instincts are in there. You just have to stop interrupting them.

## Post 06 - Worldbuilding Mistakes

**Topic:** Worldbuilding
**Best day to post:** Saturday

### LinkedIn

The most common worldbuilding mistake is not building too little.

It is building in the wrong order.

Writers spend months creating magic systems, geography, political history, and languages and then start chapter one and realize they do not know what their character wants for breakfast, let alone what drives them.

Worldbuilding serves the story. Not the other way around.

Build what your character needs to navigate. Build the culture that shaped them. Build the rules of the world that create the obstacles in their way.

Everything else can stay in your notes. Your reader will never see most of it. But they will feel whether or not the world has weight.
