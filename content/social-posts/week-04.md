## Post 19 - Story Bibles

**Topic:** Professional craft habits
**Best day to post:** Monday

### LinkedIn

TV writers have used story bibles for decades.

Before a single episode is written, there is a document. Characters, their histories, relationships, what they want, what they fear, what they look like, how they talk. The rules of the world. Timeline of events. Open threads that need resolution.

Novelists rarely use them.

It is one of the reasons TV shows with long-running casts often have better character consistency than novel series. The bible keeps everyone accountable.

If you are writing a novel, a story bible is worth building. Even a modest one. Document decisions as you make them. Not ideas, but the choices you have committed to. This character's name is now permanently this. This is what happened in their past. This is what they believe.

The document is not creative. The novel is creative. The bible just makes sure the novel stays honest with itself.

## Post 20 - On Dialogue Attribution

**Topic:** Prose craft
**Best day to post:** Wednesday

### LinkedIn

"Said" is almost always the right word.

Not "exclaimed." Not "whispered breathlessly." Not "retorted."

This is one of the most argued points in craft conversations, and the anti-"said" camp has gotten louder. But the case for "said" is simple:

"Said" is invisible. Readers skip over it and register only who is speaking. That is its job. It is not a descriptor. It is punctuation.

When you use "exclaimed," the reader pauses on the word. And usually the dialogue itself already conveys the exclamation. You are doubling up. You are telling us what the word choice already showed.

Use other dialogue tags sparingly. When you do use them, make sure they are doing something "said" genuinely cannot. "She whispered" does work that punctuation cannot. "He demanded" does not.

Trust your dialogue. It already sounds like the character. The attribution just needs to get out of the way.

## Post 21 - Plot vs Story

**Topic:** Story theory
**Best day to post:** Friday

### LinkedIn

Plot and story are not the same thing.

Plot is what happens. Story is why it matters.

You can have a thriller with an impeccably constructed plot and no story. Events happen in clever order. But nothing is at stake internally. You do not care who wins because you do not care who they are.

You can have a quiet literary novel where almost nothing happens plot-wise. But if you are watching a character reckon with something real in themselves, it is riveting.

The most durable fiction has both. External events that mirror and pressure the internal ones. A plot that is not just interesting but meaningful, because it is the specific set of pressures that forces the character to change or refuse to.

When you are planning, ask both questions: what happens, and what does it mean that it happens to this specific person.

## Post 22 - Tracking Your Manuscript's Timeline

**Topic:** Practical craft
**Best day to post:** Tuesday

### LinkedIn

One of the more practical problems in long-form fiction is timeline management.

If your story spans three months of in-world time, someone will get the days wrong. A character attends a funeral on a Tuesday. Two chapters later, they mention it was last Wednesday. Three chapters after that, it has been "about a week."

Readers with good memories will catch this. Even readers without good memories will feel it as a vague wrongness they cannot quite name.

The simplest fix: keep a timeline document. A running list of when each scene takes place. Just the date or day of the week and a one-line description. It takes seconds to maintain. It saves hours in revision.

If your story spans years, it is even more important. Characters age. Seasons change. Historical events have dates. The consistency has to hold across potentially thousands of pages.

Track time like you track characters. It is just as important.

## Post 23 - Writing in Your Own Voice

**Topic:** Voice and craft identity
**Best day to post:** Thursday

### LinkedIn

The hardest thing for a new novelist is not finding their voice.

It is trusting it.

Every writer has a voice. It is present in how they text, how they tell stories out loud, how they describe things they saw. It exists before any formal training.

Formal training is valuable. But it sometimes teaches writers to suppress their instincts rather than refine them. To write toward what sounds literary rather than what sounds true.

The writers with the most distinctive voices often cannot explain what makes them distinctive. It is just how they see things. The specific details they notice. The rhythm that feels natural.

If you are working on a first draft and your prose feels fake, try writing as if no one will read it. No performance. No professionalism. Just the thing that happened and how it felt.

The voice is usually already there. The performance is what is in the way.

## Post 24 - The Planted Object Problem

**Topic:** Plot craft
**Best day to post:** Saturday

### LinkedIn

Here is a problem that comes up constantly in long-form fiction.

You plant something in an early chapter. A detail. An object. A piece of information. You have a plan for it. You move on.

Seventy thousand words later, you have forgotten it completely. It never pays off. The reader reaches the end, looks back, and finds a thread that leads nowhere.

Or the opposite happens: you build toward a resolution in the final act that requires something to have been established earlier. But you forgot to plant it. Now you have a payoff and no setup.

Both are revision problems. Both are harder to catch than they should be because by the time you are deep in the draft, the early chapters feel like a different book.

The habit that helps: before starting a new chapter, review your list of unresolved threads. Before finishing a draft, search for every setup you remember planting. Confirm it lands.

Do not let the novel forget its own promises.
