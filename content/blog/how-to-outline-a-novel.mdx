Most writers who quit their novels do so around chapter four.

Not because the idea was bad. Not because they lacked talent. Because the story collapsed under its own weight: subplots that went nowhere, characters whose motivations stopped making sense, scenes that had no idea where they were headed.

The fix is not to write faster. The fix is to know where you are going before you start.

## Why Most Outlining Advice Fails

The classic advice is to write a synopsis. A page or two describing the story from beginning to end. The problem is that a synopsis tells you what happens, but not why it matters or how it connects. When you hit chapter four and things stop working, the synopsis gives you nothing to grip.

A useful outline is not a summary. It is a structure. It answers a specific set of questions about your story before you write a single scene.

## The Five Questions Your Outline Must Answer

Before you open a new document and start chapter one, your outline should give clear answers to these five things.

<FiveQuestionsCards />

**1. What does your protagonist want, and what are they afraid of?**

Not plot-level want. Not "she wants to find the killer." The deep want underneath that. The wound from the past that shapes every decision they make. A character without a wound is a character without an engine. The story moves because of that wound, and it resolves (or refuses to resolve) because of it.

**2. What is the worst thing that can happen to them?**

This is your climax. If you do not know what the worst possible moment looks like for your protagonist, you are driving without a destination. The climax does not have to be the most dramatic scene. It has to be the scene where everything the character has been running from finally catches them.

**3. What has to be true at the end that was not true at the beginning?**

This is your theme, made concrete. Not "the story is about redemption." Something specific: "At the end, she trusts someone again for the first time since her sister died." That sentence tells you what every scene should be building toward.

**4. What are the three turning points between beginning and end?**

These are the structural hinges. The moment the protagonist commits to the goal. The moment everything appears to be working. The moment it all collapses before the final push. You do not need to know every scene. You need to know where these pivots land.

**5. Who is making the protagonist's goal difficult, and why do they believe they are right?**

Even if your antagonist is not a person, if it is circumstance or the protagonist's own psychology, there has to be a force with its own internal logic. A conflict is only interesting when both sides make sense.

## Building the Outline Itself

Once you have answered those five questions, your outline can be short. A page, sometimes less.

The outline is not a chapter-by-chapter breakdown. It is a spine. You are writing down the skeleton so you do not lose it when the novel gets thick and confusing, and it will get thick and confusing.

Here is a simple format that works:

- **Opening image or situation:** where we meet the protagonist, and what their world looks like before the story begins
- **Inciting event:** what disrupts that world and forces them into motion
- **Turning point one:** they commit to pursuing the goal
- **Midpoint:** the apparent victory, or the deepest dive into the new world
- **Turning point two:** the collapse, the betrayal, the moment that takes everything away
- **Climax:** the worst moment, the final confrontation
- **Resolution:** what the world looks like now, and what has changed in the protagonist

That is seven beats. It fits on one page. And it gives you enough structure to write into without so much structure that the story feels predetermined.

## What to Track as You Write

The outline is the beginning, not the end. As you write, two things will drift: your characters and your subplots.

Characters will surprise you. This is good, and you should let it happen. But track it. When a character does something unexpected, write down what that means for the story's logic. Does it change their want? Does it put them in conflict with the resolution you had planned?

<CharacterDriftLog />

Subplots are where most novels lose coherence. Every subplot needs a connection to the main story's core question. If you have a romantic subplot, it should be in tension with the protagonist's wound. If you have a mystery subplot, its resolution should illuminate something about the main conflict. Subplots that exist only to add pages are the ones that make readers feel lost.

## Start Small

The biggest mistake writers make with outlines is making them too detailed. A fifteen-page beat sheet, written before you have discovered anything about the story through drafting, is a plan for a story you have not written yet. It will be wrong in ways you cannot anticipate, and wrong plans are harder to abandon than no plan.

Start with the five questions. Write one page. Then open chapter one.

The story will teach you what you need to know about the outline as you write it. Trust that process, and keep the outline close.
