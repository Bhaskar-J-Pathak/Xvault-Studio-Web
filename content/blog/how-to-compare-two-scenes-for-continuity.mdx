A continuity problem rarely announces itself as a continuity problem.

The later scene may read perfectly well on its own. The dialogue flows. The character's decision feels plausible. The object they reach for belongs naturally in the moment.

The mistake exists only in the relationship between that scene and something written many chapters earlier.

Perhaps the object was destroyed. Perhaps the character never learned the information guiding their decision. Perhaps two people now trust each other even though their last scene ended in betrayal.

You do not always need to reread the entire manuscript to investigate a problem like this. Start by comparing the two moments that should agree.

## What the Two-Passage Check Is For

The two-passage method is useful when you can identify an earlier scene that established a fact and a later scene that depends on it.

The passages do not need to discuss the fact in identical language. They only need to share a story element:

- The same character
- The same object
- The same relationship
- The same location
- The same world rule
- The same event or piece of information

The goal is not to prove that the later passage is wrong. The goal is to make the change visible enough that you can judge whether the manuscript explains it.

## Step 1: Choose the Earlier Anchor

Begin with the passage that establishes the story's earlier state.

Choose the smallest excerpt that still contains the relevant context. A few paragraphs are usually more useful than a complete chapter because they force you to identify what the continuity question actually concerns.

For example:

> Mara placed the brass key in Tomas's grave before the earth was returned. She watched until the final handful covered it.

This passage establishes more than the location of a key. It establishes that Mara intentionally surrendered it and witnessed the burial.

Write the earlier fact in plain language:

> The brass key was buried in Tomas's grave, and Mara knows it is there.

If you cannot summarize the established fact clearly, the earlier scene may be ambiguous rather than the later scene being contradictory.

## Step 2: Choose the Later Dependency

Now find the later passage that depends on the same story element.

> Mara reached into her coat and wrapped her fingers around the brass key.

Again, state what the passage assumes:

> Mara currently possesses the brass key.

The two statements do not automatically prove an error. Someone may have recovered the key. Mara may be holding a copy. The burial may have been staged.

The comparison reveals the missing question:

> Where did the key return to Mara's possession?

That is far more useful than a vague note saying “continuity issue.”

## Step 3: Compare Five Layers

Once the passages are side by side, compare them in five layers.

### 1. Story facts

List the concrete details that each passage makes true.

For an object, this might include its appearance, condition, location, owner, and last known movement. For a character, it might include physical details, abilities, injuries, titles, or family relationships.

Ask whether the later passage contradicts an earlier fact or merely adds a new one.

### 2. Character knowledge

Separate what happened from what each character knows.

An event can occur in chapter two while the protagonist remains unaware of it until chapter seven. If the protagonist acts on that information in chapter five, the event timeline may be correct while the knowledge timeline is broken.

For each passage, write:

- What does the point-of-view character know?
- How did they learn it?
- Who else knows?
- Is any belief incomplete or deliberately false?

This is especially important in mysteries, thrillers, political fantasy, and stories with multiple points of view.

### 3. Emotional state

Record the relationship or emotional state visible in each passage.

Do not reduce it to broad labels such as happy, sad, or angry. Use story-specific statements:

- She wants his approval but no longer trusts his promises.
- He is frightened of losing her and disguises that fear as control.
- They have agreed to cooperate, but neither has forgiven the betrayal.

Then ask what event connects the earlier state to the later one.

If no scene, decision, realization, or accumulation of smaller moments explains the movement, the problem may be an emotional discontinuity rather than a factual error.

### 4. Timeline

Write down every time marker, including implied ones.

Consider:

- How much time has passed?
- Could the character physically travel that distance?
- Has an injury had time to heal?
- Does the weather or season still fit?
- Did the necessary off-page event have time to occur?

A timeline problem is often hidden by smooth scene transitions. The prose moves naturally, so the impossible calendar goes unnoticed.

### 5. Open promises

Look for expectations created by the earlier passage.

The burial of the key may promise that retrieving it will be difficult, dangerous, or emotionally significant. If the key simply appears later, the problem is not only how it returned. The manuscript may also have skipped the payoff promised by the burial.

Ask whether the later passage resolves, advances, transforms, or accidentally ignores that promise.

## Step 4: Decide What Kind of Difference You Found

After the comparison, place the difference into one of four categories.

### An actual contradiction

Both passages cannot be true, and the manuscript supplies no explanation.

Fix one passage or add the missing cause.

### An intentional change with sufficient support

The fact, belief, or relationship changed, and the manuscript shows why.

No correction is needed. You may still strengthen the transition if early readers miss it.

### An intentional change with missing support

You know why the change happened, but the explanation exists only in your head or planning notes.

Add the moment that lets the reader cross the distance.

### Productive ambiguity

The apparent contradiction is part of the story. A narrator is unreliable, evidence has been falsified, or the reader is meant to question what happened.

Keep it, but make sure the uncertainty feels deliberate rather than accidental.

## Step 5: Write the Smallest Useful Revision Note

Avoid notes such as “fix continuity” or “check the key.” They force your future self to repeat the entire investigation.

A useful revision note contains the conflict and the likely missing bridge:

> Chapter 3 establishes that Mara buried the only brass key in Tomas's grave. Chapter 15 places it in her coat. Add the recovery, establish a duplicate, or change the later object.

For an emotional gap:

> Chapter 11 ends with Lio rejecting Mara's apology. Chapter 13 treats them as cautious allies. Add the decision or external pressure that makes cooperation possible while preserving the unresolved distrust.

The note should return you to a decision, not merely remind you that a problem exists.

## When Two Passages Are Not Enough

The method works best for a focused question. It becomes less reliable when the change develops gradually across many scenes.

A friendship may grow through ten small exchanges. A political alliance may depend on several promises, compromises, and private suspicions. A character's fear may change its expression without ever disappearing.

In those cases, create a short chain of evidence:

1. The passage that establishes the original state
2. Two or three scenes that apply pressure or create change
3. The later passage you are testing

You are still comparing moments, but you are allowing the manuscript to show the path between them.

## The Point Is Not Perfect Consistency

A novel should change. Characters revise their beliefs. Relationships break and heal. Objects move. Rules acquire exceptions. New information changes the meaning of earlier scenes.

Continuity work is not about freezing the story in its first state.

It is about making sure the reader can follow how one state became another.
