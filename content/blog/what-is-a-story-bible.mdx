Halfway through your second draft, you will discover that your protagonist's mother died in chapter three and is alive and well in chapter nineteen.

Or your antagonist has green eyes on page forty and brown eyes on page two hundred. Or your fictional city is three days' ride from the capital in part one and a week's ride in part two.

These are not small errors. They are symptoms of a novel written without a story bible.

## What a Story Bible Actually Is

A story bible is a living reference document that holds everything true about your story. Not what you plan to happen, but what you have decided is fixed: who these people are, where this world exists, what the rules are, what has already happened.

It is not a synopsis. It is not an outline. It is the ground truth the story stands on.

Professional television writers have used story bibles for decades. Before a single episode is written, the bible establishes the world, the characters, the tone, and the rules so that multiple writers can work on the same story without contradicting each other. Novelists typically work alone, but the problem is identical: your chapter eight and your chapter thirty need to be describing the same world.

## What Goes in a Story Bible

A story bible has no fixed format, but there are categories that most novels need covered.

**Characters**

For each significant character: their role in the story, their physical appearance (specific details you will return to), their relationship to the protagonist, their voice (how they speak, what they avoid saying), and the key decisions they make that shape the plot.

Physical details seem trivial until you describe the same person twice with different details. A brief note, grey eyes, a scar along the left jaw, speaks in short sentences, costs you ten minutes and saves you a continuity error that a reader will notice on page one of their review.

**The World**

Geography, if it matters. How long it takes to travel between places. The political or social structure, to whatever level the story requires. The rules of the world. If there is a magic system, it lives here. If there is a technology constraint, it lives here. The economy, if characters have jobs or needs that money touches.

You do not need to worldbuild everything. You need to record the decisions you have made, so you do not make different decisions in chapter twenty.

**Timeline**

When events happen relative to each other. The backstory that explains the present. Key dates if your story has them. This is particularly important in mysteries and thrillers, where the logic of the plot depends on who knew what when.

A simple chronology, even just a list of events with rough dates, will save you from time paradoxes that only become visible on your third draft.

**Unresolved Threads**

Every plot thread you have opened that has not been closed. Every promise you have made to the reader that still needs a payoff. Every question the story has raised that it still owes an answer.

This is the category most writers skip, and it is the one that creates the most problems. When you are deep in chapter sixteen, the thread you opened in chapter four is no longer visible to you. If you have not written it down, it will stay unresolved, and readers will feel its absence even if they cannot name it.

**Tone and Voice**

A few sentences about the register you are aiming for. The books you have been reading that feel like what you are writing. The emotions you want the reader to carry. This sounds like unnecessary abstraction, but when you come back to the manuscript after a two-week break, this note is the fastest way to re-enter the story's specific atmosphere.

## What Does Not Go in a Story Bible

Everything you are not yet sure about.

A story bible records decisions, not possibilities. If you have not decided whether the protagonist's father is dead or estranged, do not put either in the bible. Put the question in a notes document. Once you have written the scene that answers it, then it enters the bible.

A bible full of maybes is more confusing than no bible at all. Keep it as a record of what is fixed, and update it as you fix new things.

## When to Start

Before chapter one, if possible. Write what you know about the world and the characters. Not everything, but what you are certain of. Leave space for what you will discover in the writing.

The story bible grows as the draft grows. After each session, spend five minutes adding whatever you established that day. A character's eye color. The name of a city. A law of physics that your plot depends on. It takes five minutes in the moment and saves hours of searching the manuscript later.

## The Manual Way and the Better Way

The traditional story bible is a document, usually organized however the writer thinks best, searched by memory or Ctrl+F. This works, and many excellent novels have been written this way.

The limitation is that a manual story bible does not read the novel for you. It only holds what you have consciously added to it. The things you forget to add, the small details, the implied facts, the things that were obvious to you when you wrote them, stay invisible until they become contradictions.

As you write your chapters, it reads what you have written and extracts the facts: character appearances, relationships, locations, timelines, unresolved threads. The story bible builds itself from the manuscript, not from what you remember to record.

The writer's job is to make decisions. The story bible's job is to remember them. When the software handles the second job, you can focus entirely on the first.

## The Payoff

A novel with a well-maintained story bible is a different experience to revise than a novel without one.

Without it, revision is archaeology. You dig through the manuscript looking for where you established things, cross-checking scenes against each other, fixing contradictions that took three hundred pages to surface.

With it, revision is construction. You know what is true, you know what is open, and you can make deliberate decisions about what needs to change. The structure is visible, and visible structures can be improved.

Start the bible before you start the chapters. Keep it close. Update it every session.

Your future self in the third draft, trying to remember what colour eyes you gave the detective, will thank you.
